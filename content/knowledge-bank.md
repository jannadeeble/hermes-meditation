# Meditation Knowledge Bank

One bank of knowledge, one way of writing. When a meditation is requested, the writer reads this whole document, picks the theme, picks a practice, picks the teaching points that fit, and writes a fresh script. There is no fixed course and no day numbers. Everything the writer may say is grounded here.

## Part 1. How meditation works

### The four abilities meditation trains

Meditation trains four interacting abilities. Every session should serve at least one, and a good arc often touches two or three.

1. **Attention control.** Staying with one thing, noticing when the mind wanders, and bringing attention back. This is the return practice. It is the most basic skill and the one most sessions begin with.
2. **Body awareness.** Feeling sensations directly: weight, warmth, contact, tension, the movement of the breath. The body is a stable place to return to when the mind is busy.
3. **Emotion regulation.** Being with a feeling without being swept away by it. Watching it rise, stay, and change, instead of acting on it immediately or pushing it away.
4. **A different perspective on the self.** Seeing thoughts as events passing through, not as facts about who you are. The inner monologue is activity, not identity.

The fourth ability is the least intuitive and the most valuable for anxiety, stress, and self-criticism work. Courses that only train attention and body awareness miss it.

### Focused attention and open awareness

Two families of practice, and a session can move between them:

- **Focused attention.** Hold one anchor: the breath, the feet, a sound, a body part. When attention moves, notice and return. This builds stability.
- **Open awareness.** Let experience come and go without holding any single object: sounds, thoughts, sensations, all arising and passing. This builds the wider perspective.

Start focused, open later in the session or later in someone's practice. Guidance fades as skill grows; silence grows with it.

### What the evidence actually supports

These are the claims it is safe to speak, and no further:

- Mindfulness programs tend to produce small to moderate average reductions in anxiety, stress, and low mood after roughly eight weeks, compared with doing nothing. Results vary a lot by person.
- Some trials show small average improvements in parts of attention: staying with something, noticing distraction, flexible thinking. The effects are smaller when compared with other active training.
- Noticing that attention has wandered and choosing to return is direct practice in monitoring and redirecting attention.
- Learning to notice thoughts as events, rather than commands or facts, is a recognised part of mindfulness teaching (decentering).
- Body-focused attention and relaxation practices can ease situational anxiety in some studies, and body scan practice is associated with better noticing of body signals (interoception).
- The body's alarm response (fight or flight) is a well-documented reaction to perceived threat. Noticing it as a signal, rather than as a verdict about reality, is a teachable move.

Never claim: meditation rewires the brain in a guaranteed or simple way; a session activates a named body system or tells the body it is safe; meditation treats depression, anxiety, trauma, pain, or sleep disorders; more minutes always produce better results; a sound frequency has special healing effects; ancient use proves modern medical benefit. Human-led group research is evidence about programs, not proof that a recording does the same thing.

## Part 2. The practices

Each practice knows its cues, its forbidden cues, and its return method. The return method must match the practice. Never give a walker a breath-return cue.

### Breath anchor
Sitting or lying, attention rests on the felt movement of breathing.
- Cues: in, out, rise, fall, pause, nostrils, chest, belly, cool, warm, natural.
- Forbidden: hold your breath, force a slow rate, breathe deeply on command, control.
- Return method: return to the next felt breath.

### Body scan
Attention travels slowly through the body, part by part.
- Cues: weight, warmth, contact, tension, soften, heavy, release, heel, jaw, shoulder.
- Forbidden: none specific, but never direct into trauma or demand staying with distress.
- Return method: return to the part of the body you were resting on, then continue.

### Walking
Eyes open, attention on the felt steps.
- Cues: step, heel, toe, weight shift, path, ground, pace.
- Forbidden: close your eyes, stop watching the path, breath-return cues, any cue that asks the listener to stop seeing where they are going.
- Return method: return to the next felt step.

### Sleep and rest
Lying down, settling, not trying.
- Cues: settle, heavy, soft, release, rest, bed, pillow, blanket, dark, quiet, letting go, slowing, the day ending.
- Forbidden: stay awake, fight sleep, focus hard, concentrate, effortful language.
- Return method: return to the body's contact with the bed.
- Rule: the aim is rest and settling, not a task to succeed at. If sleep comes, that is welcome; if not, resting is enough.

### Loving kindness
Offering a direction of goodwill.
- Cues: may you be safe, may you be healthy, may you feel ease, kindness, warmth, goodwill.
- Forbidden: demanding a particular feeling, promising calm.
- Return method: return to the phrase or the wish, without needing it to feel warm.

### Sound awareness
Attention rests on hearing.
- Cues: near, far, loud, soft, sudden, steady, the silence between sounds.
- Forbidden: none specific.
- Return method: return to the next sound that arrives, or to the body as a base.

### Open awareness
Letting experience come and go.
- Cues: open, wide, space, arising, passing, sounds, thoughts, sensations, sky, room.
- Forbidden: holding on, controlling, chasing a state.
- Return method: return to the wider field, or to the body as a base when lost.

### Daily life
Bringing attention to an ordinary task.
- Cues: one thing at a time, notice, pause, choose, return.
- Forbidden: none specific.
- Return method: return to the next felt step of the task.

## Part 3. The themes

Every theme has teaching points. Each point is one plain idea, with its evidence and its return method. A session uses one theme and one to three of its teaching points, never more. Themes interlink: anxiety and sleep, stress and anger, self-compassion and everything.

### Anxiety

**A1. Anxiety is a signal, not a verdict.**
The body's alarm is built to protect. It can fire for real danger and for imagined futures alike. Noticing the alarm as a signal, rather than as a fact about the situation, is the first move.
Evidence: the alarm response is well documented; the safe wording is about noticing it as a signal, never claiming meditation treats anxiety disorder.
Return: to the body's contact with the seat, floor, or bed.
Fits: breath anchor, body scan, sleep.

**A2. Thoughts are storylines, not facts.**
The mind makes worst-case stories. You can notice the story without living inside it. The thought is an event happening now; it is not an instruction you must follow.
Evidence: decentering is a recognised part of mindfulness teaching; safe wording only.
Return: to the practice's own next felt sensation.
Fits: breath anchor, open awareness.

**A3. Anxiety lives in the body first.**
Tight chest, racing heart, butterflies, shallow breath. The body shows up before the story does. Noticing the sensation gives attention somewhere concrete to go.
Evidence: body awareness and relaxation practices show some effect on situational anxiety in studies.
Return: to the feet, the seat, or the breath.
Fits: body scan, breath anchor.

**A4. The cycle of anxious thinking.**
Worry feeds worry. The loop keeps going because the mind tries to solve the feeling by thinking about it. Noticing the loop and returning breaks the automatic cycle, once at a time.
Return: to the next felt breath.
Fits: breath anchor, open awareness.

**A5. You do not have to argue with the thought.**
The alternative to believing a thought is not fighting it. It is noticing, naming it lightly, and returning. No argument needed, no forcing thoughts away.
Return: to the body's contact or the next breath.
Fits: breath anchor, noting.

Related themes: sleep (worry at night), stress, self-compassion.

### Stress and recovery

**S1. Stress is the body mobilising for effort; recovery is the return.**
The alarm and effort response is useful for a real task. The problem is when the body never gets the signal to settle. Rest is not laziness; it is how the system recovers.
Evidence: the stress response and relaxation response are documented; safe wording only.
Return: to the body as a base.
Fits: breath anchor, body scan.

**S2. The pause between demand and response is yours.**
Something happens, then you act. Between them is a gap. The practice is widening that gap so the response is chosen, not automatic.
Return: to the next felt breath before responding.
Fits: breath anchor, daily life.

**S3. Strain shows up in the body early.**
Shoulders, jaw, shallow breathing, gripping. Catching strain early is easier than calming it late. The body scan trains this early noticing.
Return: to the part of the body carrying the tension.
Fits: body scan.

**S4. Recovery can be one small deliberate pause.**
Not a long holiday, not a perfect day. One minute of settling, one walk without a phone, one slow breath. Small recoveries add up.
Return: to the next felt step of the pause.
Fits: daily life, walking.

Related themes: anxiety, sleep, anger.

### Sleep

**N1. Rest is not a test.**
There is nothing to succeed at. If sleep comes, welcome it. If it does not, resting is still the practice. Trying to force sleep pushes it away.
Evidence: safe wording only; sleep is not treatable by a recording.
Return: to the body's contact with the bed.
Fits: sleep.

**N2. The day ends; it does not need solving now.**
The mind reopens the day's questions at night. The practice is not to solve them, but to notice the urge to solve and return to the bed.
Return: to the body's contact with the bed.
Fits: sleep.

**N3. Settle the body one part at a time.**
Heavy legs, soft hands, a quiet jaw, the weight resting into the mattress. Body-first settling gives the mind less to hold.
Return: to the next part of the body.
Fits: sleep, body scan.

**N4. Effort and ease.**
Enough effort to remember the practice, enough ease to keep returning. Too little drifts; too much strains. The middle is alert, gentle, and willing to begin again.
Return: to the body's contact with the bed.
Fits: sleep, breath anchor.

**N5. If sleep comes, that is welcome; if not, resting is enough.**
This is the whole stance of the sleep practice. State it plainly, once, early.
Return: to the body's contact with the bed.
Fits: sleep.

Related themes: anxiety (worry at night), stress.

### Anger

**G1. Anger is information, not an order.**
It points at a boundary, a threat, or an injustice. The feeling is real and valid. It does not have to become an action.
Return: to the body, the feet, the breath.
Fits: breath anchor, body scan.

**G2. The early cues come first.**
Heat, tightness, clenching, a rising pressure. If you can notice the first cue, you have more room than if you wait until the wave is full.
Return: to the place in the body where the heat or tightness is.
Fits: body scan.

**G3. There is a gap between trigger and response.**
You can widen it. Feel the anger, name it, and choose. Feeling it fully and acting on it are different things.
Return: to the next felt breath.
Fits: breath anchor, daily life.

**G4. You can feel anger without pretending it is not there.**
The alternative to lashing out is not suppressing. It is acknowledging the feeling and letting it pass through the body without directing it at anyone.
Return: to the body as a base.
Fits: open awareness, body scan.

Related themes: stress, relationships, self-compassion.

### Self-compassion and the inner critic

**C1. The inner critic is a voice, not the truth.**
The self-critical voice was built to protect, and it still runs. You can hear it without agreeing with it, the same way you hear any other thought.
Return: to the breath or the body.
Fits: breath anchor, open awareness.

**C2. Kindness is an intention, not a demanded feeling.**
You can offer goodwill even when warmth does not arrive. The practice is the willingness to offer it, not a mood to produce.
Return: to the phrase or the wish.
Fits: loving kindness.

**C3. Talk to yourself like a friend.**
The tone of the practice is the tone you would use with someone you care about. Notice the inner tone and soften it, without forcing.
Return: to the next felt breath.
Fits: breath anchor, loving kindness.

**C4. Three parts of self-kindness.**
Be kind to yourself; remember you are not alone in struggling; hold the difficulty in mindful awareness. All three together, none demanded.
Evidence: self-compassion research (Neff); safe wording only.
Return: to the phrase or the body.
Fits: loving kindness, breath anchor.

Related themes: anxiety, anger, grief.

### Focus and attention

**F1. Noticing the wander is the practice.**
Attention wandering is not failure. Realising it has wandered is already a moment of awareness, and choosing to return is the training.
Evidence: attention-return card, moderate evidence for small attention improvements.
Return: to the chosen anchor.
Fits: all practices.

**F2. An anchor is a place to return, not a grip.**
Hold the breath or the feet lightly. The point is not to lock attention in place by force; it is to have somewhere to come back to.
Return: to the anchor.
Fits: breath anchor, body scan, walking.

**F3. Sensation before story.**
First there is the felt thing: pressure, warmth, movement, sound. Then the mind adds names and opinions. You can notice the sensation without joining the story.
Return: to the raw sensation.
Fits: body scan, breath anchor, open awareness.

**F4. Narrow and wide.**
Attention can rest on one detail, then widen to the field around it. Narrow makes the anchor clear; wide includes body, sound, and space while the anchor stays available.
Return: to the anchor, then widen again.
Fits: open awareness, breath anchor.

Related themes: stress, all.

### Relationships

**R1. Reacting versus responding.**
Someone acts, the alarm fires, and the reaction is automatic. The practice is finding the pause, feeling the reaction, then choosing the response.
Return: to the next felt breath before speaking.
Fits: breath anchor, daily life.

**R2. You can hear someone without fixing or winning.**
Listening is not agreeing and not defeating. It is being present with what another person says while holding your own ground gently.
Return: to the body as a base.
Fits: breath anchor, open awareness.

**R3. Empathy starts with your own reaction.**
Before you can meet someone else's state, notice your own: your tightness, your story about them, your urge. Then there is room for theirs.
Return: to the body.
Fits: body scan, open awareness.

**R4. Kindness offered without demanding warmth.**
Offer goodwill toward the person, whether or not the feeling flows. The offering is the practice.
Return: to the phrase or the wish.
Fits: loving kindness.

Related themes: anger, self-compassion, stress.

### Grief and change

**H1. You do not have to move on quickly.**
Allowing is a practice. Grief is not a task with a deadline. The practice is to be with what is here, gently, for this moment.
Return: to the body as a base.
Fits: breath anchor, open awareness.

**H2. Feelings come in waves.**
A wave rises, peaks, and passes. You can ride one without drowning in it, and without needing it to stop immediately.
Return: to the next felt breath.
Fits: breath anchor, body scan.

**H3. This hurts, and you are still here.**
Two things can be true at once. Holding both is steadier than forcing cheerfulness or sinking into the hurt.
Return: to the body, the ground, the room.
Fits: open awareness, breath anchor.

**H4. Care for yourself the way you would care for a friend.**
The same kindness you would offer someone else grieving is available to you. Not forced, just offered.
Return: to the phrase or the body.
Fits: loving kindness, breath anchor.

Related themes: self-compassion, sleep.

## Part 4. The writing rules

These rules govern every script. The writer reads them every time.

- One session, one theme, one practice, one plain theme detail. The theme is an everyday literal detail (a kitchen in the early evening, rain on the window, the walk from the door to the bed), mentioned once or twice, never a metaphor and never the star.
- Conversational register. Write the way a calm, kind friend talks to one person. No poetry, no mysticism, no cliches, no metaphors, no aphorisms, no poster lines, no inspirational maxims.
- No line that tells the listener what they must feel. No promises of calm.
- The wandering response is always notice and return. Never tell the listener to let a thought go, to drop it, or to empty the mind. Thoughts come and go on their own; the practice is noticing and coming back.
- The return method must match the practice. Breath returns to the next breath, walking to the next step, sleep to the bed's contact.
- No repeated instruction, even reworded. Each cue is spoken once.
- No invented weather, time, surroundings, history, or local facts.
- Do not tell the listener to hold their breath, force a slow rate, or do breathwork.
- Rising distress moves attention outward: sight, sound, contact with the ground, the current room. Never direct into trauma. Panic, flashbacks, or severe distress means offering a simple grounding session or human support, never an intense inward practice.
- The writer chooses where the longer pauses go and how long each is. Ordinary lines keep the fixed gap, the welcome keeps its 3-second gap on its own short line, and punctuation within a line gets a real pause.
- The welcome is its own short line (max two words). A merged "Welcome. <first line>" makes the pause artificially tiny.
- Use at least three delivery tones across a session, beginning with settling and ending with closing. Keep the whole arc calm. For sleep, nothing energetic: no brightening, no lift.
- A session ends with transfer to daily life, not a sales-style conclusion.

## Part 5. How a session gets written

1. The request names the practice (or lets the writer choose), the length, the theme (or lets the writer choose), and sometimes the teaching point (or lets the writer choose from the theme).
2. The writer reads this whole bank, then selects: one theme, one to three teaching points from it, one practice, one plain theme detail.
3. The writer builds the arc: arrival (settle the body), teaching (state the one idea plainly, then why it matters), practice (apply it), wandering return (notice and return with the practice's exact return method), deepening (same practice, fewer words), integration (connect to daily life), closing (widen and end simply).
4. The writer respects the length budget: speech fills part of the time, silence fills the rest, longer sessions mean more silence, not more talking.
5. The script is checked against the practice's cues, forbidden words, and return method, and against the writing rules. Then, and only then, is it voiced.
